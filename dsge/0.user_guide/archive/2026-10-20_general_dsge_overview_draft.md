# General DSGE training user guide: overview and model summary

Draft prepared 20 October 2026. Suggested replacement text for the overview and model summary of the user guide. Monetary policy and financial stability applications remain placeholders for future additions.

## Overview

This document is a practitioner’s guide to implementing and using dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models for policy analysis with Dynare, running in MATLAB or GNU Octave. It provides the necessary information to specify, solve, estimate, and analyse these models, while developing the skills to maintain and improve them. The practitioner should have a basic understanding of macroeconomic theory and DSGE models, together with familiarity with MATLAB or Octave. Supplementary training resources are provided under References, including a short MATLAB primer, model code, and practical exercises.

DSGE models provide a structural framework for analysing how economic shocks and policy decisions affect the economy over time. They describe the decisions of households, firms, and policymakers, and how these decisions interact across markets. This allows practitioners to identify the mechanisms through which a policy operates, distinguish the sources of economic fluctuations, and evaluate alternative policy scenarios. Depending on the model specification, these applications can include fiscal and monetary policy, financial stability, and the distributional and welfare effects of policy interventions.

The DSGE framework encompasses a family of models rather than a single specification. Models used in policy institutions commonly build on a New Keynesian framework, incorporating nominal and real frictions to explain business cycle dynamics and the transmission of policy. Extensions introduce features such as international trade and capital flows, unemployment, financial intermediaries, and differences in household income, wealth, and access to financial markets. The appropriate specification depends on the policy question, the economic environment, and the available evidence. A relatively simple model can help identify a transmission mechanism, while a more detailed model may be required to assess its quantitative importance (Yagihashi, 2020; Cahn, Fève & Matheron, 2026).

For South Africa, open-economy and financial mechanisms are particularly relevant when analysing the transmission of domestic and foreign disturbances. Hollander & van Lill (2020), for example, extend a benchmark New Keynesian model to incorporate financial market features and international linkages, and use the resulting models to study the sources of South African business cycle fluctuations. This illustrates how a common modelling framework can be adapted to address different questions while retaining a consistent account of the underlying economic mechanisms.

The guide begins with a high-level summary of a typical DSGE model. It then provides the steps to install and configure Dynare with MATLAB or Octave, create the model code, interpret the Dynare output and workspace, and perform basic analysis. Subsequent sections cover estimation with time series data and the baseline commands and options for sensitivity and identification analysis, forecasting, and optimal policy analysis. Key code syntax and results are explained throughout. Practical sections also cover Dynare troubleshooting, the macro processing language, and the production of LaTeX output.

To supplement these exercises, the guide draws on research applications, including models developed for South Africa. These illustrate the use of impulse response functions, historical shock decompositions, fiscal multipliers, policy counterfactuals, and conditional forecasts. Fiscal applications include changes in the composition of taxation and expenditure, alternative debt stabilisation rules, and persistent fiscal transfers. Replication files accompany the relevant applications.

While DSGE models provide a coherent framework for policy analysis, their results remain conditional on the mechanisms, parameter values, policy arrangements, and data incorporated in the model. Structural interpretations therefore require careful identification, empirical validation, and sensitivity analysis. In practice, DSGE models form part of a broader toolkit that includes other macroeconometric models, institutional knowledge, and policy judgement.

The document concludes with best practices for model maintenance and improvement, including version control, documentation, collaboration, and model validation. The FAQs and accompanying resources will be updated as common questions arise during workshops and as further modelling and forecasting applications are developed.

## Summary of a typical DSGE model

Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models describe an **environment** in which economic **agents**, such as households and firms, make decisions according to specified **behavioural assumptions**, subject to resource, technological, and institutional constraints. These decisions interact through markets for goods and services, labour, capital, and financial assets. An equilibrium requires that agents’ decisions are mutually consistent and satisfy the model’s market-clearing conditions and accounting relationships.

The framework links the economy’s long-run properties to its short- and medium-run dynamics. In many applications, a steady state or balanced growth path provides the reference point around which economic fluctuations are analysed. Shocks move the economy away from this reference point, while agents’ responses, economic frictions, and policy arrangements determine the subsequent adjustment. A balanced growth path allows relevant variables to grow at stable rates; after suitable transformations, the model can often be expressed around a stationary steady state.

The following features describe a typical New Keynesian DSGE model used for policy analysis. They provide a common starting point, with additional detail introduced where required by the application.

1. **Environment.** The model is dynamic because decisions and their consequences extend across periods, stochastic because the economy is subject to uncertainty, and general equilibrium because outcomes reflect interactions across markets. The environment specifies the available goods and assets, production technologies, market structures, and policy institutions. Depending on the application, the economy may be closed or open to international trade and financial flows.

2. **Behaviour and expectations.** Households typically choose consumption, saving, and labour supply to maximise expected lifetime utility, while firms choose production, investment, and prices to maximise profits or firm value. These decisions are forward-looking: current choices depend on expectations of future income, prices, returns, and policy. Rational expectations are a common benchmark, although alternative specifications can incorporate learning, imperfect information, or bounded rationality.

3. **Households.** Households receive income from labour, asset holdings, firm ownership, and government transfers, and allocate their resources between consumption and saving. A representative-household model provides a tractable starting point. Extensions distinguish households by their income risk, wealth, borrowing constraints, or access to assets. A two-agent specification, for example, may separate households able to save and borrow from households that consume their current disposable income. Richer heterogeneous-agent models allow the distribution of income and wealth to influence aggregate outcomes. These distinctions matter for the transmission of interest rate changes, taxes, and transfers, and for evaluating distributional and welfare effects.

4. **Firms and production.** Firms produce goods and services using labour and, where included, private and public capital. New Keynesian models commonly distinguish monopolistically competitive producers of differentiated intermediate goods from competitive firms that combine these goods into final products. Market power allows firms to set prices above marginal cost, while nominal rigidities make prices sticky. Models with physical capital also describe investment, depreciation, and the accumulation of productive capacity. The number of sectors and the treatment of production depend on the question being studied.

5. **Economic frictions.** Nominal frictions, such as sticky prices and wages, allow changes in nominal spending and interest rates to affect real economic activity in the short run. Real frictions can include consumption habits, investment adjustment costs, variable capital utilisation, and labour market search and matching. These features influence the size and persistence of economic responses. Their inclusion and parameterisation should be supported by evidence and assessed through sensitivity analysis.

6. **Fiscal policy.** The fiscal authority purchases goods and services, makes transfers, raises revenue, and issues or repays debt. More detailed specifications distinguish public consumption and investment, and taxes on consumption, labour, and capital income. Fiscal rules describe how instruments respond to economic conditions and government debt. The government budget identity links revenue, expenditure, and financing in each period; the policy specification must also provide a coherent account of how government liabilities are financed over time. Fiscal outcomes therefore depend on the financing instrument, the persistence of policy changes, household behaviour, and the monetary policy response.

7. **Monetary policy.** The monetary authority commonly sets a short-term nominal interest rate according to a Taylor-type rule, responding to inflation and a measure of economic activity, often with gradual interest rate adjustment. Policy affects consumption, investment, production, and inflation through expected real interest rates and, in open-economy models, exchange rates. Extensions can incorporate alternative policy regimes, an effective lower bound on interest rates, asset purchases, or optimal policy. The interaction between monetary and fiscal arrangements is important for determining equilibrium outcomes.

8. **Financial markets and financial stability.** A simple benchmark may abstract from financial intermediation and assume that borrowing and saving take place without significant financial frictions. Extensions introduce banks, borrowing constraints, collateral, credit spreads, and borrower or intermediary balance sheets. These mechanisms allow financial conditions to amplify and propagate economic shocks. Models intended for financial stability policy analysis must also specify the relevant sources of financial vulnerability and the instruments used to address them, such as capital requirements or limits on borrowing.

9. **Foreign economy.** Open-economy models incorporate international trade, exchange rates, and cross-border asset holdings. Domestic outcomes can then respond to foreign demand, international interest rates, commodity prices, and changes in external financing conditions. The foreign economy may be represented by a small set of exogenous processes, a compact structural model, or an explicitly modelled trading partner. The appropriate treatment depends partly on whether the domestic economy is assumed to have a negligible influence on international conditions.

10. **Shocks and empirical implementation.** Shocks represent unexpected changes in factors such as productivity, preferences, mark-ups, policy instruments, or external conditions. Parameters are calibrated, estimated, or determined using a combination of approaches. The model’s equations and solution are then used to generate simulations, impulse responses, historical decompositions, forecasts, and policy counterfactuals. Interpreting these results requires attention to the mapping between model variables and observed data, parameter identification, and the suitability of the solution method for the experiment.

A typical DSGE model therefore combines a common economic structure with features selected for the application. The specification should balance the detail needed to capture relevant transmission mechanisms with the tractability needed to explain and evaluate the results. More detail does not automatically produce more reliable policy conclusions. The model’s usefulness depends on the question it addresses, its empirical support, and the robustness of its results to plausible alternative assumptions.

## Editorial notes

The existing Kemp & Hollander (2020) description would fit well at the start of the fiscal applications section, where its detailed household, tax, expenditure, and public capital features directly motivate the exercises.

The draft retains the author's progression from economic motivation to mechanisms and practical applications, concrete examples, explanations of technical terms, and a balanced account of the benefits and limitations of structural modelling.

The three source papers contribute complementary elements:

- **Yagihashi (2020):** institutions use different models for different purposes; modelling choices and parameter values need empirical justification.
- **Cahn, Fève & Matheron (2026):** the framework connects theory, data, and policy experiments, with financial and household heterogeneity extensions used where the question requires them.
- **Hollander & van Lill (2020):** a progression from a benchmark model to financial and open-economy extensions, with structural decompositions providing a concrete South African application.
